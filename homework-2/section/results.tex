The results show a consistent progression from lexical retrieval toward neural ranking. The best retrieval-only configuration reaches an MRR@5 of 0.557, while the strongest reranked run reported in Table~\ref{tab:main-results} reaches 0.702. The largest observed step comes from adding
\\\texttt{nvidia/llama-nemotron-rerank-1b-v2}: the top-1000 Nemotron run reaches an MRR@5 of 0.702 and Recall@100 of 0.911, compared with 0.557 and 0.839 for the retained hybrid retriever. We do not report confidence intervals, paired significance tests, or effect sizes for these aggregate comparisons, so they should be read as observed metric differences only. Due to limited computing resources and time constraints, we were not able to run the same setting on the full English training dataset; it would have required more than 40 hours of computation on our RTX 3090 for the top-k = 1000 reranking.

For the runs performed on the \texttt{dev\_set}, we provide a separate multilingual check rather than a single aggregate score. On the English development split, the non-fine-tuned Nemotron baseline reaches 0.675 MRR@5 and 0.835 Recall@100 when reranking the top 100 candidates; it reaches 0.692 MRR@5 and 0.909 Recall@100 when reranking the top 1000 candidates. The fine-tuned Nemotron LoRA run on the same English development split reaches 0.716 MRR@5 and 0.913 Recall@100. The corresponding fine-tuned development runs reach 0.76 MRR@5 and 0.929 Recall@100 for French and 0.67 MRR@5 and 0.889 Recall@100 for German with the top-2000 candidate setting.

\subsection{Evaluation Evidence and Metrics}\label{sub:results-evidence}

We report three complementary forms of evidence. First, we use the TRAIN English run-analysis split to compare retrieval and reranking configurations over 14,977 queries. This is the largest labeled split available to us and is therefore the most reliable source for per-query statistical analysis. Second, we validate the final fine-tuned reranker on the multilingual DEV splits: English, French, and German. Third, we evaluate the final official TEST submission; this lets us report effectiveness metrics for the final multilingual runs.

The primary metric is MRR@5, following the task objective. We also report Recall@100 to measure whether the correct paper remains in the candidate set, and MAP and nDCG@10 to describe the quality of the ranking beyond the first reciprocal-rank hit. Since each query has a single gold publication in the available labeled files, per-query MRR@5 is zero unless the gold paper appears in the first five positions, and is $1/r$ when the first relevant paper appears at rank $r \leq 5$.

All modified parameters are reported in Table~\ref{tab:run-comparison}.
The hybrid runs differ in the linear weights assigned to dense, sparse, and ColBERT-style BGE-M3 signals. The reranking runs use the retained hybrid candidate generator \texttt{H513} and rerank increasingly deep candidate pools with \texttt{nvidia/llama-nemotron-rerank-1b-v2}. The $\Delta$ column is computed against \texttt{H513}, because this is the hybrid configuration used by the submitted reranking pipeline.

The weakest configuration among the reported runs is \texttt{H424}, with MRR@5 of 0.5067. Increasing the dense component while keeping a moderate sparse contribution is beneficial: \texttt{H513} improves MRR@5 by 0.0500 over \texttt{H424} and also reaches the best Recall@100 among the hybrid-only runs. \texttt{H315} has a marginally higher MRR@5 than \texttt{H513}, but its Recall@100 is lower. For this reason, \texttt{H513} is the more appropriate candidate generator for the full pipeline, where the goal of retrieval is to expose as many plausible documents as possible to the reranker.

The largest improvement is introduced by neural reranking. Moving from \texttt{H513} to \texttt{N1000} increases MRR@5 from 0.5567 to 0.7018, an absolute gain of 0.1451 and a relative improvement of 26.1\%. The gain is not only an early-rank effect: Recall@100 also rises from 0.8392 to 0.9109 when reranking a deeper candidate pool. The difference between \texttt{N500} and \texttt{N1000} is much smaller in MRR@5, only 0.0008, but \texttt{N1000} keeps a higher Recall@100. This indicates diminishing returns in early precision after roughly 500 reranked candidates, while deeper reranking still helps recover relevant documents in the tail.

Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction on per-query MRR@5 corroborate these observations. Specifically: \texttt{H315} vs \texttt{H513} is not significant (Holm-adjusted $p=1.0$). By contrast, \texttt{H513} outperforms \texttt{H424} with a highly significant difference (Holm-adjusted $p\approx 8.58\times10^{-116}$). The improvement from \texttt{H513} to the Nemotron top-1000 reranker is both large and highly significant (mean $\Delta=0.1451$; Holm-adjusted $p$ underflowing to 0). The difference between \texttt{N500} and \texttt{N1000} is statistically detectable after Holm correction (Holm-adjusted $p\approx 0.04974$) but its practical magnitude on MRR@5 is negligible ($\Delta\approx 0.0008$), which supports the "diminishing returns" interpretation. For Recall@100 the H513 vs N1000 gain is also significant (Holm-adjusted $p\approx 3.46\times10^{-195}$).

\subsubsection{Rank-bucket and error analysis}

Table~\ref{tab:rank-bucket-analysis} reports the rank position of the gold document for the retained hybrid and reranked runs. The analysis separates three qualitatively different error types. A gold paper is counted as \textit{missing} only when its gold \texttt{pubkey} is absent from every document available in the ranked file for that query. Thus, for runs with retained deep candidate lists, missing cases indicate that the reranker cannot recover the document from its input pool. For runs available only as submitted top-100 lists, missing means absent from the available top-100 file, not necessarily absent from the original upstream retrieval pool. A gold paper appearing after rank 100 has been retrieved by the retained reranking pool but lies outside the Recall@100 cutoff. A gold paper appearing between ranks 6 and 100 is present in the candidate evidence but is ranked too low to satisfy the top-5 objective.

The labeled English comparison shows that the main effect of reranking is to move correct papers to the very top of the list. The best reranked run on the TRAIN split places the gold paper at rank 1 for 9697 queries, compared with 7459 for the retained hybrid single-stage run. It also reduces missing cases from 2409 to 929. The remaining errors are informative: for \texttt{Nemotron Baseline}, 1291 queries have the gold paper in ranks 11--100, indicating ranking failures rather than retrieval failures, whereas 929 queries remain missing from the available reranking pool and therefore cannot be recovered by the reranker.

The final English DEV and TEST runs follow the same pattern.\ \texttt{Nemotron LoRA fine-tuned} places the gold paper at rank 1 for 2574 of 3905 queries, and does so also for the TEST set with 3950 of 6076 queries. Their top-5 coverage is similar, with 3112 DEV queries and 4876 TEST queries resolved within the first five ranks. The DEV file contains 1000 ranked documents for each query, so the 99 cases in \texttt{Rank $>100$} correspond to gold papers found by the retained reranking pool but not placed within the first 100 positions. The TEST file used to compute the metrics contains only the submitted top-100 documents per query; consequently, \texttt{Rank $>100$} is zero by construction, and the 465 missing TEST cases should be interpreted as missing from the available submitted run rather than as evidence that the original upstream retrieval pool never retrieved the gold paper.

Overall, the bucket analysis indicates that reranking substantially improves early precision while leaving two residual failure modes. The first consists of ranking failures, where the gold paper is present but remains below the top-5 cutoff. The second consists of missing cases, which are more severe because they cannot be repaired by reranking alone. These missing cases are likely associated with severe vocabulary mismatch, translation drift, or claims whose wording emphasizes entities and contextual cues that are weakly represented in titles and abstracts.

\subsubsection{Error patterns and failure analysis}

We manually inspected missing cases as a qualitative diagnostic. The following patterns are not mutually exclusive and should be interpreted as plausible failure modes rather than a measured taxonomy:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Claim-to-metadata specificity mismatch:} Some claims mention specific findings, medical outcomes, or statistical results that are not present in the indexed title and abstract metadata. These cases are difficult for the first-stage retriever because the query emphasizes details unavailable in the indexed document representation.

    \item \textbf{Translation artifacts in non-English queries:} In French and German runs, some translated queries contain domain-term shifts, awkward literal translations, or untranslated technical expressions. These artifacts can reduce lexical and semantic alignment with the English title--abstract corpus.

    \item \textbf{Implicit references with little lexical overlap:} Some claims refer to a paper indirectly, using phrases such as ``this study'' or ``these papers'' without naming concrete findings, authors, institutions, or venues. In these cases, the social-media wording provides too little evidence for reliable candidate retrieval.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Performance by query characteristics}

Figure~\ref{fig:query-characteristics-mrr} reports a query-level characterization of the \texttt{Nemotron Baseline} run on the English TRAIN split. Contrary to our initial intuition, token length alone is not positively associated with higher MRR@5 in this run: short queries with at most 20 tokens reach 0.760 MRR@5, medium-length queries reach 0.713, and long queries with at least 35 tokens reach 0.672. This suggests that longer tweet-like claims often add conversational or contextual material that does not necessarily help identify the target paper.

The named-entity cue analysis is more consistent with the retrieval intuition. Using a lightweight heuristic based on author-like capitalization patterns, acronyms, and institution or venue keywords, entity-rich queries reach 0.733 MRR@5, while entity-sparse queries reach 0.664. These observations remain exploratory and should be interpreted as heuristic patterns rather than formal statistical findings, but they support the value of hybrid retrieval that combines lexical anchors with semantic matching.
