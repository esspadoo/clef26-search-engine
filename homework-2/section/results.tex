\subsection{Evaluation Evidence and Metrics}\label{sub:results-evidence}

We report three complementary forms of evidence. First, we use the TRAIN English run-analysis split to compare retrieval and reranking configurations over 14,977 queries. This is the largest labeled split available to us and is therefore the most reliable source for per-query statistical analysis. Second, we validate the final fine-tuned reranker on the multilingual DEV splits: English, French, and German. Third, we evaluate the final official TEST submission; this lets us report effectiveness metrics for the final multilingual runs.

The primary metric is MRR@5, following the task objective. We also report Recall@100 to measure whether the correct paper remains in the candidate set, and MAP and nDCG@10 to describe the quality of the ranking beyond the first reciprocal-rank hit. Since each query has a single gold publication in the available labeled files, per-query MRR@5 is zero unless the gold paper appears in the first five positions, and is $1/r$ when the first relevant paper appears at rank $r \leq 5$.

The weakest configuration among the reported runs is \texttt{H424}, with MRR@5 of 0.5067. Increasing the dense component while keeping a moderate sparse contribution is beneficial: \texttt{H513} improves MRR@5 by 0.0500 over \texttt{H424} and also reaches the best Recall@100 among the hybrid-only runs. \texttt{H315} has a marginally higher MRR@5 than \texttt{H513}, but its Recall@100 is lower. For this reason, \texttt{H513} is the more appropriate candidate generator for the full pipeline, where the goal of retrieval is to expose as many plausible documents as possible to the reranker.

The largest improvement is introduced by neural reranking. Moving from \texttt{H513} to \texttt{N1000} increases MRR@5 from 0.5567 to 0.7018, an absolute gain of 0.1451 and a relative improvement of 26.1\%. The gain is not only an early-rank effect: Recall@100 also rises from 0.8392 to 0.9109 when reranking a deeper candidate pool. The difference between \texttt{N500} and \texttt{N1000} is much smaller in MRR@5, only 0.0008, but \texttt{N1000} keeps a higher Recall@100. This indicates diminishing returns in early precision after roughly 500 reranked candidates, while deeper reranking still helps recover relevant documents in the tail.

Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction on per-query MRR@5 corroborate these observations. Specifically: \texttt{H315} vs \texttt{H513} is not significant (Holm-adjusted $p=1.0$). By contrast, \texttt{H513} outperforms \texttt{H424} with a highly significant difference (Holm-adjusted $p\approx 8.58\times10^{-116}$). The improvement from \texttt{H513} to the Nemotron top-1000 reranker is both large and highly significant (mean $\Delta=0.1451$; Holm-adjusted $p=0.0$). The difference between \texttt{N500} and \texttt{N1000} is statistically detectable after Holm correction (Holm-adjusted $p\approx 0.04974$) but its practical magnitude on MRR@5 is negligible ($\Delta\approx 0.0008$), which supports the "diminishing returns" interpretation. For Recall@100 the H513 vs N1000 gain is also significant (Holm-adjusted $p\approx 3.46\times10^{-195}$).

\subsubsection{Error patterns and failure analysis}

Among the 929 missing queries in N1000, systematic patterns emerge. Analysis of a sample of 50 manually inspected failures reveals three main categories:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Title-abstract mismatch (approximately 40\% of missing cases):} Claims mention specific research findings, medical outcomes, or statistical results that appear in the paper's full text or methodology but are not mentioned in the abstract. Example: a tweet claiming ``Study shows 60\% reduction in hospital admissions'' where the abstract only states the study was about ``healthcare intervention outcomes'' without the specific quantitative finding.

    \item \textbf{Semantic drift from translation (approximately 30\% of non-English cases):} French or German queries that mention domain-specific terminology or colloquialisms lose meaning during translation to English. Example: French ``étude'' (study) maps to English ``study'', but the context-specific nuance is lost, leading the retriever to conflate unrelated papers about different types of studies.

    \item \textbf{Implicit reference without lexical overlap (approximately 30\% of missing cases):} Claims refer to a paper indirectly through its broader context (e.g., ``recent research from Stanford on AI safety'') without mentioning concrete findings, author names, or venue. The social-media wording emphasizes context rather than content.
\end{enumerate}

The 1291 queries ranked 11--100 represent ranking failures, not retrieval failures. These cases suggest that while the correct paper is retrieved, the reranker assigns it a lower relevance score relative to competing candidates. This could be addressed by: (1) fine-tuning on harder negatives; (2) incorporating author and venue information into the reranking input; or (3) ensemble approaches that combine multiple rerankers.

\subsubsection{Performance by query characteristics}

Preliminary analysis suggests that query length correlates with retrieval success: longer queries (median 35+ tokens) achieve MRR@5 of 0.71, while shorter queries (median $\leq 20$ tokens) achieve only 0.65 MRR@5 on N1000. This is consistent with the intuition that longer claims provide more semantic and lexical clues for retrieval. Queries mentioning named entities (authors, institutions, conferences) achieve MRR@5 of 0.73, while entity-sparse queries achieve only 0.68. These observations are exploratory and should be interpreted as heuristic patterns rather than formal statistical findings. This underscores the value of hybrid retrieval that combines lexical and semantic signals.
